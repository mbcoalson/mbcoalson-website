I do not know whether artificial intelligence will bring us a post-scarcity society.

It could make knowledge, design, analysis, and routine labor so inexpensive that the material conditions of life change profoundly. Or it could make powerful institutions more efficient at extracting value. Neither outcome is inevitable.

We do not need to promise technological salvation to pursue a **less cruelly scarce world**.

Where do cost, opacity, and institutional indifference make ordinary life harder than it needs to be? Where can new tools, paired with public-minded systems, give people more freedom?

Freedom is often discussed as a matter of formal rights or consumer choice. Both matter. Neither is sufficient.

A person may be free to choose among ten health insurance plans while lacking the time, expertise, money, or bargaining power to get care. A family may be free to buy a more efficient home while the information needed to understand their house remains expensive, unreliable, or reserved for large commercial projects. A renter may be free to move while rising costs, work, childcare, and a lack of housing make that “choice” fictional.

These are not failures of individual character. They are failures of social structure.

The question is not whether people have options in the abstract. It is whether they have meaningful control over the conditions of their lives.

That is why I keep returning to energy modeling. In my work, I see the value of understanding how a building performs: where it loses heat, how equipment interacts, what improves comfort, what lowers operating costs, and where a sensible-looking upgrade creates a problem elsewhere.

That analysis takes expertise, detailed inputs, and time. Major projects can justify the expense. For existing homes and smaller buildings, the cost often puts it out of reach. People make consequential decisions with partial information, contractor rules of thumb, or whichever sales narrative arrives first.

AI may change that. I can imagine accurate, transparent energy modeling available for every building for little more than the cost of compute. Not as an oracle or a replacement for engineers, contractors, or field verification. Buildings have messy histories, hidden conditions, human occupants, and constraints no model can wish away.

But inexpensive modeling could put good questions within everyone's reach.

What is driving this home's discomfort? Which improvements matter? What are the tradeoffs between cost, carbon, resilience, maintenance, and comfort? What should be measured before spending money? Which claimed savings are plausible, and which are hand-waving?

That is an expansion of practical freedom.

If the cost of understanding a building falls dramatically, ignorance can no longer masquerade as inevitability. A homeowner can ask better questions. A renter can understand whether a building is healthy and affordable to live in. A city or utility can identify where assistance would make the largest difference. Schools, nonprofits, and small businesses can access analysis once reserved for organizations with the biggest budgets.

Then comes the harder question:

> If we can understand the energy performance of every building at nearly no cost, what will we build?

We could build a better market for optimizing property portfolios, pricing risk, and selling premium services to people who already have choices. There is nothing wrong with efficiency or profit. But that would be a narrow use of a broad capability.

Or we could turn cheap expertise into broad capability: accessible retrofit guidance, public programs that pair analysis with funding, stronger building standards, tenant protections, workforce training, trustworthy local technical assistance, and financing that does not punish people who cannot absorb an upfront cost.

The difference is not in the intelligence of the model. It is in the values of the institutions around it.

The same choice extends beyond buildings. AI can make education more adaptive or give affluent students attentive tools while everyone else gets automated compliance software. It can reduce bureaucracy or make denial systems faster and more impersonal. It can expand access to legal, medical, technical, and financial knowledge—or reserve that access as a premium feature.

Technology gives us capacities. It does not decide who gets to use them, who owns the systems that deliver them, or whose interests they serve.

This is where discussions of wealth become more than irritation at someone's expensive car.

I do not believe a good society requires gray sameness. People should be able to love beautifully made things, pursue unusual interests, build excellent businesses, and enjoy the rewards of difficult work. A crafted object can embody care, artistry, engineering, or delight. Abundance should include pleasure.

But abundance should not become a contest in domination.

The problem with extreme wealth is not the private jet or the dozen homes. It is that wealth can become sovereignty: the power to purchase insulation from the society that made it possible, then purchase control over that society in return.

When a few people control housing, employment, media, infrastructure, marketplaces, data, and political influence, their wealth shapes the freedom available to everyone else.

No individual should be able to purchase exemption from the society that made their wealth possible—or purchase dominion over it.

That is not an argument against prosperity. It is an argument against private fiefdoms.

A less cruelly scarce world would raise the floor of ordinary life before celebrating the ceiling of private excess. It would make sufficiency dignified: a safe home, useful education, reliable care, time with family, culture, mobility, and a chance to participate in public life. It would count good schools, parks, libraries, transit, clean air, resilient infrastructure, and shared knowledge among its greatest achievements.

The test for AI should not be whether it makes the powerful more capable. Of course it will. The test is whether it gives more people meaningful control over their lives.

We may never reach post-scarcity. We do not need to wait for utopia to decide that needless insecurity is a moral failure—and that our growing technical capacity should make freedom real for more people.
